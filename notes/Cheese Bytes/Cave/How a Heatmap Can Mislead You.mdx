Imagine this: you want to open a small coffee shop in your city. You do not want
to choose the location by instinct alone, so you gather a dataset with hundreds
of signals: delivery orders, searches for "coffee near me", and places where
people often buy breakfast.

At first the data does not look useful at all. It is just a cloud of points. You
can tell that some areas are busier than others, but you cannot yet point to a
crisp recommendation and say: "Open here".

# Why We Aggregate

This is exactly why heatmaps and choropleths feel so appealing. They promise to
turn a messy cloud into a clean surface.

The usual recipe is simple:

1. Lay a grid on top of the city.
2. Count how many events fall inside each cell.
3. Paint each cell with a color scale.

Suddenly the map feels much easier to read. We do not see isolated orders
anymore. We see regions, hot spots, and apparently clear recommendations.

<HeatmapAggregationVisual client:only="react" />

This step is not wrong. Aggregation is often useful. The problem is that the
result now depends on something that was never in the raw data: **the geometry
of the grid itself**.

# The Grid Is Part of the Model

When people look at an aggregated map, they often treat the cells as if they
were natural neighborhoods. But those boundaries are artificial. We made them
up.

That means the map is not just showing demand. It is showing demand **after we
forced it through a particular partition of space**.

Shift the partition a little and some points cross a border. A cell that looked
dominant can lose part of its count. Another one can suddenly absorb a nearby
cluster and look far more important.

<HeatmapShiftComparisonVisual client:only="react" />

This is the heart of the **Modifiable Areal Unit Problem**, or **MAUP**:

> The pattern you think you see can depend heavily on how you grouped the same
> underlying spatial data.

Nothing about the customers changed. Nothing about the orders changed. Only the
aggregation scheme changed. But the story changed anyway.

# Three Quiet Choices That Change the Story

Once you notice MAUP, it becomes hard to unsee. There are at least three quiet
decisions hiding inside a heatmap like this:

- **Origin**: where the grid starts.
- **Scale**: how large each cell is.
- **Shape and orientation**: squares, hexagons, and rotated tessellations do not
  pool points in the same way.

Those choices sound cosmetic, but they affect which observations become
neighbors and which ones get separated by a border.

The explorer below lets you perturb those choices directly. Drag the grid. Make
the cells bigger. Rotate them. Swap squares, triangles, hexagons, or even
postcode-like administrative districts. The underlying point cloud stays the
same, but the summary surface keeps mutating.

<HeatmapExplorerVisual client:only="react" />

Postal codes deserve a special mention because they feel so official. But they
were designed to make delivery and routing easier, not to reveal clean
behavioral tribes. The same warning can apply at the level of states or even
countries: a border may be administratively convenient while still being a poor
match for how people actually behave.

So if a pattern appears only because one postcode map, one state map, or one
country split happened to cut space that way, that pattern is already fragile.
The explorer's postcode mode is there to make that point concrete: changing the
administrative shape alone can change the story.

This is why a choropleth or heatmap should be treated as a **modeling output**,
not as raw truth. The picture depends on assumptions, even when those
assumptions are easy to miss.

# Not Just a Spatial Problem

This is not only a map problem.

The deeper issue appears whenever we choose an arbitrary unit of aggregation and
then forget that it was a choice. Spatial cells are one example, but time
buckets can do the same thing. Administrative units can do the same thing.
Product categories, age bands, school districts, sales territories, or postal
codes can all end up acting like artificial containers that shape the story.

Once we group data, the boundary starts participating in the result.

# A Year Is Also A Boundary

Take the S&P 500 at monthly resolution. The underlying series is just a long
sequence of monthly values. But the moment we summarize it as "yearly"
performance, we introduce a hidden modeling choice:

> When does the year begin?

Calendar years begin in January, but that is a convention, not a law of the
market. A fiscal year could start in April. A reporting cycle could start in
July. The monthly data is identical, yet the 12-month buckets shift.

The visual below keeps the same monthly history and only moves the place where
the year starts. Each row is still twelve months. But some years flip from
positive to negative, or the other way around, simply because the boundary
moved.

<TemporalAggregationVisual client:only="react" />

This is the temporal analogue of the MAUP in geospatial analysis: we start
believing that the aggregated year is a natural object, when in fact it is a
frame we imposed on a continuous sequence.

# Arbitrary does not mean meaningless.

A calendar year is a convention, but it is not random. People and institutions
organize behavior around it: holidays, planning cycles, reporting habits.
December and January are not just identical months with different names.

The same applies to geography. Postal codes, census tracts, or neighborhood
boundaries may reflect real differences in how places are built, governed, or
lived in. Sometimes a boundary really does separate two different environments.

But that does not make the boundary neutral.

The key point is that aggregation requires a choice. And different defensible
choices can produce different results from the same underlying data. A pattern
can look strong under one set of boundaries, weak under another, and sometimes
disappear entirely.

That is the heart of the MAUP: the problem is not that boundaries are always
wrong. The problem is that they matter.

# Can Smoothing Help?

A common response is to smooth the counts after aggregation. That is often a
reasonable thing to do. If neighboring cells are meant to describe the same
continuous urban process, some local averaging can reduce visual noise and make
broader patterns easier to read.

One popular option is a Gaussian filter. Instead of using only the count in one
cell, we replace it with a weighted average of nearby cells, giving more weight
to closer neighbors.

That can produce a much cleaner surface. But it does **not** make MAUP go away.
Smoothing happens **after** we already chose the grid. It can soften the sharp
edges of aggregation, but it still inherits the partition we started from.

# What To Do in Practice

The practical lesson is not "never aggregate." It is this:

1. Treat aggregation choices as assumptions.
2. Check whether your conclusions survive shifts in origin, scale, cell shape,
   year start, or administrative boundary.
3. Report patterns that remain stable across reasonable groupings.

If a recommendation appears only for one lucky tessellation, it is probably not
strong enough to trust.

That is what makes MAUP important. It reminds us that a spatial summary is not
just a lens over the data. It is a lens that can bend the image.
